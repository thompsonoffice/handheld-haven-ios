HANDHELD HAVEN
Your one-stop shop for handheld device information.

An accessible plain text encyclopedia of handheld and small Windows computers, from 2004 pocket PCs and the original GPD Win to the ROG Xbox Ally X, written for blind and low vision buyers.

WHAT THIS IS
Handheld Haven is a collection of plain UTF-8 text files. There are no tables, images or special formatting. Every heading is in capital letters on its own line, so you can find it with your screen reader's find command or by reading line by line. Every device entry starts with a line that begins with the word ENTRY and a colon, so searching for "ENTRY:" jumps from device to device.

WHY IT EXISTS
Some handheld PCs cost less than a laptop, and some have a keyboard built in. For a blind person, the questions that matter are rarely in spec sheets. Can I turn on a screen reader without sighted help? Can I feel which button is which? Will my audio games run? Handheld Haven collects what is publicly documented, says plainly when something is not documented, and tries to help you decide whether a handheld is worth buying.

HOW TO READ IT
Start with 01-INDEX.txt for the list of files and devices. Read 03-blind-buyers-guide.txt if you are deciding what to buy. Read 04-screen-reader-setup.txt before setting up a device. Each numbered family file covers one maker or product line. Handheld-Haven-complete.txt contains everything in one file, in order, for people who prefer a single document.

EACH DEVICE ENTRY HAS THESE LABELED LINES
The entry line, which starts with ENTRY and a colon, then the device name.
Maker. Released. Launch price (with currency, source and year where found).
What it can do: a plain overview of its capabilities.
A hardware section: processor, memory, graphics, storage, display, battery, weight and size, operating system, Wi-Fi and network, Bluetooth, cellular, ports and expansion, other hardware.
Controls and button layout.
How it feels: a physical, tactile description drawn from reviews.
Blind usability: Narrator, NVDA and JAWS, how to start a screen reader, controller navigation, audio, setup and maker software.
Game compatibility: performance tier, known issues, anti-cheat and audio games.
Uncertain: anything disputed, unclear, or drawn from weaker sources.
Sources.

HONESTY NOTES
"not documented" means no source used for this book stated it. It does not mean the feature is missing. UNCERTAIN means a source was weak or unclear. DISPUTED means sources disagree, and both values are given. THIN ENTRY marks devices with little reliable information. Prices are launch prices from the dates shown, not current prices; prices change, and some later rose. Nobody involved in writing this book tested these devices with a screen reader. Blind usability notes come from published reviews, forum reports from blind users, and Microsoft and maker documentation, and are credited where used.

HOW IT WAS MADE
Handheld Haven was compiled with AI assistance from public sources, then structured for screen readers. See 07-build-notes.txt for the method and the known limits, and 06-sources.txt for every web address used. Check anything important, especially prices and compatibility, before you buy.

HISTORY AND ERAS OF HANDHELD WINDOWS COMPUTERS

This chapter is an overview. Details and sources are in each device entry.

ERA 1: POCKET PCS AND PROJECT ORIGAMI, 2004 TO 2010
The OQO model 01 shipped in October 2004 with a Transmeta Crusoe processor, a slide-out keyboard and full Windows XP in a body under a pound. In spring 2006 Microsoft and Intel launched the Ultra-Mobile PC, code-named Project Origami: small touchscreen PCs, usually 7 inches or smaller. Samsung's Q1 (suggested retail US$1,099), Sony's VAIO UX (US$1,800 for the UX180P) and Fujitsu's U810 (US$999) were typical. Prices stayed near laptop prices, batteries lasted a few hours, and Windows XP and Vista were not built for tiny touchscreens. Netbooks and smartphones overtook them. OQO stopped operating in 2009, and Sony's ultra-wide VAIO P ended in 2011.

ERA 2: WINDOWS 8 TABLETS, 2012 TO 2016
Windows 8 brought a touch-first interface. Microsoft released its own Surface tablets in 2012: the Surface RT, which ran Windows RT on an ARM chip and could not install normal desktop programs, and the Surface Pro, which ran full Windows. Intel's Bay Trail Atom chips made cheap 8-inch Windows tablets possible in late 2013: Dell's Venue 8 Pro (US$299.99), Toshiba's Encore (US$329), and in 2014 the HP Stream 7 at just US$99. These tablets had a hardware Windows button, which matters for blind users because Windows button plus Volume Up is Microsoft's touch-device shortcut for starting Narrator. Dell ended the Venue line in 2016.

ERA 3: CROWDFUNDED GAMING HANDHELDS, 2016 TO 2022
GPD crowdfunded the GPD Win in 2016: an Atom x7-Z8700 machine shaped like a Nintendo 3DS-style clamshell with a game controller and thumb keyboard, running full Windows 10. GPD followed with the Win 2, Win Max, Win 3 and Win 4, and pocket laptops like the GPD Pocket and MicroPC. AYANEO (from 2021) and One-Netbook's ONEXPLAYER (from 2021) joined with larger controller-shaped devices using Intel Core and AMD Ryzen chips. These devices were expensive, sold mostly online through crowdfunding, and aimed at enthusiasts.

ERA 4: THE MAINSTREAM HANDHELD BOOM, 2022 TO TODAY
Valve's Steam Deck (February 2022, from US$399) showed there was a big market, though it runs SteamOS, a Linux system. ASUS launched the Windows-based ROG Ally in 2023 at US$599.99 to US$699.99, Lenovo the Legion Go, and MSI the Claw in 2024. AMD's Z1, Z1 Extreme and Z2 chips were designed for handhelds. In 2025 ASUS and Microsoft released the ROG Xbox Ally and Xbox Ally X, with Microsoft's Xbox full screen experience, a console-style mode that boots to a game launcher. Microsoft also made Xbox mode (Win+F11) available on other Windows handhelds. Prices rose through 2025 and 2026; the Xbox Ally X launched at US$999.99, and Valve raised Steam Deck OLED prices in May 2026.

ACCESSIBILITY THROUGH THE ERAS
In the UMPC era, screen readers such as JAWS ran on Windows XP, and NVDA's first versions appeared in 2006, but no reviews of blind use of UMPCs were found. In the Windows 8 era, Narrator gained touch gestures, and an 8-inch tablet with a Windows button could, in principle, be started and used entirely by touch. In the gaming handheld era, the controller replaced the keyboard, and that made screen reader setup harder: most handhelds have no Windows button and no keyboard, so plugging in a keyboard is the most reliable way to turn on Narrator. In 2025 Valve added a screen reader to SteamOS (in beta), and reviewers and blind users began publishing their experiences with the ROG Xbox Ally X.

BLIND BUYER'S GUIDE: SHOULD YOU BUY A HANDHELD?

This guide is for blind and low vision people deciding whether a handheld Windows computer is worth it, and which kind. It is based on the entries in this book. No device was tested by the author. Prices are launch prices with the year shown; they are not today's prices, and some later went up. Check current prices yourself.

FIRST QUESTION: WHAT DO YOU WANT IT FOR?
If you want a small computer for writing, email, the web and screen reader use, the most important feature is a physical keyboard. A handheld with a built-in keyboard, or a tablet with a keyboard cover, gives you every screen reader command. A controller-only gaming handheld is a poor main computer without an external keyboard.
If you want to play mainstream games on the go, a controller-shaped handheld like the ROG Ally or Legion Go makes sense, but plan on a small Bluetooth keyboard for setup and for screen reader work.
If you want to play audio games (games made to be played by sound), almost any Windows handheld from the last several years is more than powerful enough, because most audio games need very little processing power. What matters more is that the device runs normal 64-bit Windows on an Intel or AMD processor, has a headphone jack or good Bluetooth audio, and works with your screen reader.

CAN A HANDHELD REPLACE A LAPTOP?
Some can, partly. A handheld with a Ryzen or Intel Core processor runs full Windows, NVDA, JAWS and Office like a laptop. Most handhelds have USB-C ports that can connect to a dock with a keyboard, monitor and power. The tradeoffs: tiny or no keyboards, short battery life when gaming, fans that can be loud, and maker software that may not be screen reader friendly. A US$599.99 ROG Xbox Ally (2025 launch price) plus a keyboard can cost less than many gaming laptops, but it is not cheaper than a basic laptop for office work.

STARTING A SCREEN READER: THE BIG DIFFERENCE BETWEEN DEVICES
Microsoft documents two ways to start Narrator: Windows key+Ctrl+Enter on a keyboard, and Windows button+Volume Up on a touch device with a hardware Windows button.
Devices with a built-in keyboard (GPD Win, Win 2, Win Max, Win Max 2, Win Mini, GPD Win 4 with its slide-out keyboard, GPD Pocket and MicroPC, AYANEO Flip KB) let you press Windows key+Ctrl+Enter yourself, where the keyboard has a Windows key and Ctrl key (check the entry; on tiny keyboards some keys need Fn). The GPD Win 3 is a trap here: its slide-out keyboard is a flat touch panel with no physical keys.
Small Windows 8 era tablets (Dell Venue 8 Pro, Toshiba Encore, HP Stream 7) have a hardware Windows button.
Controller-only handhelds (ROG Ally family, Legion Go family, MSI Claw, most AYANEO and OneXPlayer models, Zotac Zone) do not list a Windows button. Can I Play That's review of the ROG Xbox Ally X found Windows setup mentions only keyboard combinations to turn on Narrator. Plan to plug in a USB or Bluetooth keyboard for first setup, or have sighted help for the first few minutes.

VALUE AND BUDGET PICKS (BASED ON LAUNCH PRICES AND DOCUMENTED FEATURES)
These are suggestions to research further, not tested recommendations. Every price below is a launch price, dated.

Best value for a screen reader user who also games: ROG Xbox Ally, US$599.99 at launch in October 2025. It runs Windows 11 with Xbox mode, and the best accessibility reporting in this book is for its sibling, the Xbox Ally X: an AppleVis user found NVDA and Narrator workable in Xbox mode and Armoury Crate "surprisingly accessible", and Can I Play That documented button remapping with macros. Add a keyboard for setup. The original ROG Ally (US$599.99 Z1, US$699.99 Z1 Extreme, 2023) shares the same Armoury Crate software and is often discussed as a cheaper used option, but no used price was sourced.

Best value with a built-in keyboard for gaming: GPD Win Mini (crowdfunding from US$699 in 2023, retail about US$820) or GPD Win 4 (from US$799 in 2022). Both have a keyboard for screen reader commands and a game controller. GPD's own software accessibility is not documented.

Best small productivity machine: GPD Pocket 3 (from US$650 on Indiegogo in 2021 with a Pentium Silver; higher at retail) or GPD Pocket 4 (newer and more powerful, about US$1,300 to US$1,400 as tested by Notebookcheck). These are pocket laptops with keyboards, full Windows, and in the Pocket 4's case strong performance. Notebookcheck's price widget showed Pocket 4 Amazon listings on November 12, 2026 (see the entry), but prices change constantly.

Budget Windows tablet with a keyboard: Surface Go line. The Surface Go launched at US$399 in 2018 (keyboard extra, US$99 for the Go 2 keyboard). These are Intel x86 tablets, so NVDA and JAWS install normally once you leave S mode, a free one-way switch in Settings. They are not gaming machines. The Surface Go 4 for Business launched at US$579 in 2023.

Cheapest ever new: HP Stream 7, US$99 in 2014. Today it is a curiosity: 1 GB of RAM and 32 GB of storage, far too little for a modern screen reader to run comfortably.

Steam Deck: from US$399 at launch in 2022 (LCD), with OLED prices raised to US$789 and US$949 on May 27, 2026. SteamOS now has a screen reader for the Steam interface (2025 beta), but Windows audio games and NVDA need Windows, which SightlessKombat installed only with sighted help. Choose it only if Steam games are your main goal and you accept those limits, or have help installing Windows.

WHAT TO AVOID
Windows RT devices (Surface RT and Surface 2): they cannot install NVDA, JAWS or normal desktop programs. Only Narrator works.
Very old devices for daily use: UMPCs and 2013 Atom tablets cannot run current NVDA well, and many are 32-bit.
ARM Windows devices (Surface Pro X, Surface Pro 11 Snapdragon models, Surface Pro 12-inch) for gaming: NVDA runs through emulation and JAWS has an ARM64 version, but anti-cheat and some older games and drivers do not run on ARM. They are fine for productivity.
MSI Claw if maker software accessibility matters to you: an AppleVis user reported MSI Center is not accessible (single report; may have changed).

USED-MARKET NOTES
No used-market prices were collected for this book, so none are given. A Reddit discussion by a blind ROG Ally owner and AppleVis forum posts mention buying handhelds, but without prices we could verify. If you buy used:
Ask the seller to confirm the battery holds a charge and the device boots to Windows without a password or account lock. A device still linked to someone's Microsoft account or with BitLocker on may be hard to reset without sighted help.
Ask whether the joysticks drift (move by themselves), a common wear problem on handhelds.
Ask whether the charger is included; many handhelds need a USB-C charger of a specific wattage.
Confirm the exact model and memory. For example, the original ROG Ally came in Z1 and Z1 Extreme versions, and the Surface Go came in 4 GB and 8 GB versions.
Plan to reset Windows yourself with a keyboard connected, then turn on Narrator during setup with Windows key+Ctrl+Enter.
Buy from sellers with a return window when you can.

CHECKLIST BEFORE YOU BUY
Does it have a keyboard, or will you carry one?
Is it x86 (Intel or AMD), so NVDA, JAWS and audio games run normally?
Does it run full Windows 10 or 11, not Windows RT, and is S mode removable?
Is there a 3.5 mm headphone jack, or are you happy with Bluetooth audio latency?
Can the maker's software remap a button to a keyboard shortcut? (Documented for ASUS Armoury Crate.)
Is the battery life acceptable for what you will do? Gaming handhelds often last one to three hours under load.
Is there a return policy, in case setup is harder than expected?

Sources for claims in this guide are in the matching device entries and in 06-sources.txt.

SCREEN READER SETUP ON HANDHELD AND SMALL WINDOWS DEVICES

This chapter collects documented steps. It was not tested on each device. Microsoft's Narrator documentation and the reviews cited are the sources.

STEP 1: GET A KEYBOARD READY
For any handheld without a built-in keyboard, connect a USB keyboard (a USB-C adapter or hub may be needed) or a Bluetooth keyboard. A USB keyboard works during Windows setup without pairing. Bluetooth pairing during setup without speech is difficult.

STEP 2: TURN ON NARRATOR DURING WINDOWS SETUP
Press Windows key+Ctrl+Enter (Microsoft Support, Narrator keyboard commands). On a touch device with a hardware Windows button, Microsoft documents holding the Windows button and pressing Volume Up. Can I Play That found Narrator is available right away in setup on the ROG Xbox Ally X, but the on-screen prompt only mentions keyboard combinations, not controller buttons.
The Steam Deck is different: it ships with SteamOS. Valve added a SteamOS screen reader in a June 2025 beta (The Verge), and Windows setup on a Deck needed sighted help in SightlessKombat's review because audio drivers are missing during setup.

STEP 3: MAKE NARRATOR OR NVDA START AUTOMATICALLY
In Narrator settings, turn on "Start Narrator before sign-in for everyone" and "Start Narrator after sign-in" (Microsoft Support, customizing Narrator). After installing NVDA, NVDA's own options can start it at Windows sign-in.

STEP 4: XBOX MODE (FULL SCREEN EXPERIENCE)
On the ROG Xbox Ally family and, from late 2025, on other Windows 11 handhelds, Microsoft offers Xbox mode, also called the full screen experience. Press Win+F11 to enter and leave it. Microsoft says that when Xbox mode is set to start at sign-in, other startup apps start only the first time you switch to the desktop. To make sure NVDA runs, set NVDA to Start at log in under Settings, Apps, Startup (Microsoft Support, KB5070297). An AppleVis user with five months on the ROG Xbox Ally X found Xbox mode navigable with NVDA or Narrator.

STEP 5: MAP A BUTTON TO A SCREEN READER SHORTCUT
Maker software can often turn a back paddle or spare button into a keyboard key or macro. Can I Play That reports ASUS Armoury Crate SE can remap every button except the Xbox, Command Center and Library buttons, with macros of up to five key presses. Use this for keys such as Insert or Caps Lock (the Narrator and NVDA modifier key), Tab, Escape, or a combination. Whether a specific combination like Windows key+Ctrl+Enter is accepted was not confirmed in sources. Lenovo Legion Space, MSI Center, AYANEO AYASpace and OneXConsole accessibility is not well documented; one AppleVis user reported MSI Center as not accessible.

STEP 6: USE TOUCH WHEN NO KEYBOARD IS ATTACHED
Narrator supports touch gestures on touchscreens (Microsoft Support appendix): touch or drag to hear what is under your finger; flick left or right to move to the previous or next item; double-tap to activate; three-finger swipes to move by Tab and Shift+Tab; and four-finger triple-tap shows all gestures (check Microsoft's current list, as gestures change between Windows versions). NVDA also supports touch gestures on touchscreens. SightlessKombat found NVDA touch input not very responsive on a Steam Deck running Windows, and a keyboard "worked wonders".

STEP 7: ARM DEVICES AND S MODE
ARM Windows devices (Surface Pro X, Snapdragon Surface Pro 11 and Pro 12-inch): NVDA runs through Microsoft's x64 emulation, and JAWS provides a native ARM64 build (Freedom Scientific system requirements). Windows RT (Surface RT and Surface 2) cannot install NVDA or JAWS at all.
Surface Go and some low-cost devices ship in Windows S mode, which only installs Store apps. Switch out of S mode (free, one-way) in Settings before installing NVDA.

STEP 8: AUDIO
Most handhelds have a 3.5 mm headphone jack; check the entry. Bluetooth headphones add delay, which matters for audio games. A USB-C audio adapter or USB audio interface is an alternative on devices without a jack.

STEP 9: KEEP A RECOVERY PLAN
Windows updates and maker software updates can reset settings. Keep a keyboard with you for the first weeks, and learn how to restart Narrator with Windows key+Ctrl+Enter if NVDA stops.

ENTRY: GPD Win (original)
Also known as: GPD Win 1
Maker: GamePad Digital (GPD), Shenzhen, China
Released: Announced October 2015; crowdfunded on Indiegogo in 2016; shipped from October 2016 (Wikipedia).
Launch price: Indiegogo pre-order backers were offered US$330 (Wikipedia) and Liliputing reported a US$299 early crowdfunding price; planned retail was US$499 (Laptop Mag via search pointer, UNCERTAIN); Liliputing's October 2016 review noted US$340 from GPD's AliExpress store.

WHAT IT CAN DO
A full x86 Windows 10 computer the size of a large Nintendo DS. It runs ordinary Windows desktop programs, older and lighter PC games, emulators and audio games, and it has a QWERTY keyboard built in. It is not powerful enough for modern 3D games.

HARDWARE
Processor: Intel Atom x7-Z8700 (first units shipped with x7-Z8750, then GPD standardized on the Z8700 because it performed better, per Liliputing). Quad-core, four threads. The Z8700 is the same chip Microsoft used in the Surface 3, whose Wikipedia infobox lists 1.6 GHz base and up to 2.4 GHz, 2 W scenario design power.
Memory: 4 GB LPDDR3, soldered (not upgradeable).
Graphics: Intel HD Graphics 405 (integrated). Compute units: not documented.
Storage: 64 GB eMMC 4.51 (soldered) plus one microSD slot.

FILE: 05-glossary.txt
====================

GLOSSARY

Each term is on its own line, followed by its meaning.

ANTI-CHEAT: Software that some online games use to block cheating. Some anti-cheat systems do not work on ARM Windows or on SteamOS.
APU: A chip combining the processor and graphics, used in AMD handhelds (for example Z1 Extreme).
ARM: A processor design used in phones and in Snapdragon and SQ Windows devices. Many older Windows programs run through emulation on ARM.
ARMOURY CRATE SE: ASUS's control software on ROG Ally handhelds.
AUDIO GAME: A game designed to be played by sound alone, popular among blind players.
BAY TRAIL: Intel's 2013 Atom chips used in cheap Windows tablets.
CLAMSHELL: A device that opens like a laptop or a Nintendo DS.
COMPUTE UNITS (CU): Groups of processing cores in an AMD graphics chip. More usually means faster graphics.
CROWDFUNDING: Pre-selling a product on sites like Indiegogo before it is made. Many GPD, AYANEO and OneXPlayer devices launched this way.
DOCK: A stand or hub that connects a handheld to a monitor, keyboard, power and network.
eGPU: External graphics card, connected through USB4, Thunderbolt or a special port such as OCuLink.
EMMC: Slower soldered storage used in budget devices.
FULL SCREEN EXPERIENCE: See XBOX MODE.
GYRO: A motion sensor used for aiming in games.
HALL EFFECT: Magnetic joystick or trigger sensors that resist drift.
HAPTICS: Vibration feedback.
JAWS: A commercial Windows screen reader from Freedom Scientific (Vispero).
LPDDR5 / LPDDR5X: Fast, low-power memory, usually soldered and not upgradeable.
MICROSD: A small memory card slot for extra storage.
NARRATOR: The screen reader built into Windows. Start or stop it with Windows key+Ctrl+Enter.
NVDA: NonVisual Desktop Access, a free, open-source Windows screen reader.
OCULINK: A fast external port used by some GPD and AYANEO devices for external graphics.
ORIGAMI: Microsoft and Intel's 2006 project code name for Ultra-Mobile PCs.
PRISM: Microsoft's x86 and x64 emulator on ARM Windows.
S MODE: A locked mode of Windows that only installs Store apps. It can be turned off for free, one time.
SOC: System on a chip.
STEAMOS: Valve's Linux-based system for the Steam Deck and some Legion Go S models.
TDP: Thermal design power, roughly how many watts a processor is allowed to use. Handhelds often let you change it.
THUMB KEYBOARD: A small keyboard typed with the thumbs.
THUNDERBOLT / USB4: Fast USB-C standards supporting displays, docks and sometimes external graphics.
UMPC: Ultra-Mobile PC, a 2006 to 2010 category of small Windows computers.
WINDOWS HELLO: Windows sign-in with a fingerprint or face camera instead of a password.
WINDOWS RT: A 2012 to 2013 version of Windows for ARM that could not install normal desktop programs or third-party screen readers.
XBOX MODE: Microsoft's console-style full screen Xbox app experience on Windows 11 handhelds, toggled with Win+F11.

BUILD NOTES

HOW THIS BOOK WAS MADE
Handheld Haven was compiled in November 2026 with AI assistance. The process: fetch reviews, maker pages, Microsoft documentation and Wikipedia articles; extract facts into structured notes; write each entry only from those notes; mark gaps as "not documented"; and generate the text files with a script so every entry has the same labeled lines. Hunter Thompson (thompsonoffice) chose the subject, scope and title.

WHAT WAS NOT DONE
No device was handled or tested. No screen reader testing was performed by the author. Blind usability notes come from Microsoft documentation, Can I Play That, AppleVis forum posts, SightlessKombat, The Verge and a Reddit post, credited where used. Game compatibility tiers are general guidance from processor and graphics class plus reviewers' reports; they are not benchmark results. No used-market prices were collected. Launch prices are historical, with their year; current prices were not tracked except where an entry gives a dated price.

SOURCE RULES
Facts come from fetched pages where possible. Facts that came only from a search engine summary are marked UNCERTAIN. Where sources conflict, both are given and marked DISPUTED. No specifications, prices, quotations or tests were invented. There are no download links of any kind.
